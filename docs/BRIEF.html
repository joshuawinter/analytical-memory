Analytical Memory · brief for enterprise architects

Your warehouse now drafts your metric definitions. Nothing records who settled them, or why.

Analytical Memory is a self-hosted system of record for what your numbers mean. It finds rival definitions of the same measure, gets a dated human ruling with a reason, keeps every ruling in an append-only ledger, and answers people and agents from the ruling in force at the moment they ask.

The gapInduced semantic layers keep whichever definition is most common.
The controlA human ruling, with its rationale, outranks frequency.
The assetAn auditable history of how the enterprise decided what its numbers mean.
The ownershipIt stays yours. Models borrow it one question at a time. Nothing is trained in.
01 · The failure mode

Same metric, same city, same week, two numbers.

In 2021 Uber's data platform team reported that "Shopping Session" read 6.53 million in one tool and 6.20 million in another. One tool carried outdated filters. Their fix was a certified metric store where someone writes each definition by hand. Nobody published the step before that: noticing from usage that two definitions were in circulation, and recording who settled it and why.

02 · Why now

The analyst who used to catch the wrong filter is leaving the loop.

When the analyst's tool was SQL, the definition sat in a WHERE clause a colleague could read and question. That informal review was the control. In chat, the person asking never sees the SQL, and the definition is buried in code nobody reads.

SQL eraChat and agent era
Who asksAn analyst who writes the queryA business user who types a question
Where the definition livesIn SQL a colleague can readIn generated SQL nobody reads
Who catches a wrong onePeer review, the next analyst, the report ownerNobody, unless the decision is recorded
Reach of one mistakeOne dashboardEvery answer every agent gives
Who drafts the semantic layerA person, by handThe warehouse, from the majority of past queries

Better models fix the SQL, not the decision.

Two definitions of store sales can both be valid SQL. Which one your company chose is a decision, and decisions are not in the data. As models get better at writing SQL, a larger share of the remaining errors are about meaning. The benchmarks already show it: BEAVER, built on real enterprise warehouses, reports 10.8% accuracy for agentic frameworks, rising to 30.1% only when the domain knowledge is supplied.

A supervised correction loop.

People label only the disputed cases. The memory keeps each label with its reason, and every later question reads it. Nothing is retrained, so each correction applies at once, to every model, and can be revoked. The expected effect is that answer accuracy improves as rulings accumulate. That effect is the claim the pilot is designed to measure.

03 · Where it sits

An additive layer between the evidence you already have and everything that consumes meaning.

The warehouse stays the system of record for data. Analytical Memory becomes the system of record for meaning. It reads the drafts your platform already produces and publishes approved definitions back out. It replaces nothing.

Induction from query history is the platforms' job and an input here. The product is the part after it: the fork, the ruling, the ledger and the time-aware answer.

05 · The control

Every answer carries one of four labels.

The label decides how an answer is presented, never whether the analyst sees one. No number is presented as official until a named person has ruled.

UnruledShow every answer, none official.

Nobody has ruled. If rival definitions are in use, each one's number comes back side by side with the person who decides. The more popular one gets no special treatment.

ApprovedAnswer from the ruling.

A named person ruled, with a reason and an effective date. The ruling outranks frequency.

Not approvedStill visible, clearly marked.

The rival the owner ruled against. It stays answerable on request, so the analyst can see what changed and why.

SupersededAnswers for its own period.

A later ruling replaced it. Questions about the time it governed still get it, and its history stays readable.

06 · The ruling record

A verdict without a reason teaches nothing.

"Sales definition wins" settles one case. "These differ by grain, not definition; both are valid, disambiguate by date basis" settles the next thousand without a human. So the rationale is mandatory, and the ruling is stored as data the agent reads at question time. Nothing is trained into a model, so a ruling can be inspected, corrected and revoked.

The ledger is plain markdown with flat front matter. An auditor can read it with a text editor, and any edit made outside the ledger is detected.

07 · Time

Ask as of any date and get the answer that was governing then.

When finance re-rules in April, February's reports still have to be explainable under February's ruling. Rulings carry an effective date and are resolved against the moment the question is about. This is the scenario the Tier 5 test runs.

In the test, ruling 2 reverses ruling 1 to prove that a later decision replaces an earlier one without erasing it. Today an unruled measure returns its definitions without numbers; returning both numbers is designed. One time axis today: when a ruling takes effect. Separating "when it was true" from "when we recorded it" (full bitemporal history) is a design question still open.

Approval is a decision. Confidence is earned over time.

Approval is not a probability: a named person decided, or nobody has. If it were a likelihood, frequency would be back in charge. What does vary is how far to trust a standing ruling. It rises as later usage conforms and results validate. It falls as teams diverge, challenges pile up, or the ruling ages.

Below a threshold the definition reopens and the owner is asked again. Confidence decides when to ask. It never decides the answer.

Without ground truth nobody can measure the probability that a definition is correct. What can be measured is how well usage agrees with the ruling, over time. Designed, not built: it runs on the conformance checks in section 09.

How the analyst drills down

  1. AnswerEvery number in use, each labelled unruled, approved, not approved, or superseded.
  2. DefinitionWhat each number counts, in plain words.
  3. DecisionWho decides, who decided, when, and why.
  4. EvidenceWho uses each definition, how often, and how far apart the numbers are.
  5. Data pathThe tables and SQL. Only for someone who needs to check it.

Who decides

Every measure needs a named owner, and naming them is the customer's first piece of work in a pilot. Analysts can challenge a definition. Only the owner rules, in their own words, under their own identity. The model never approves anything.

When nobody decides

If the owner does not respond, or two owners claim the same measure (finance and sales over revenue is the classic case), the fork goes to a tie-breaker the customer names. Until someone rules, every answer stays labelled unruled. Nothing becomes official by default.

The label travels with the number

Copied, exported or quoted, an unruled figure carries its label and the name of the person who decides. A contested number pasted into a deck without its label is the original failure again.

What reaches Anthropic

The question, the matching memory entries and the answers for that one question, under your Claude Enterprise agreement and recorded by hash. The rest of the memory, the ledger, the warehouse and the conformance checks stay inside your boundary.

Illustrative. Today the server runs locally over stdio and a contested measure returns its definitions without numbers. Returning every answer labelled, labels that travel, tie-breaker routing, a remote connector with SSO identity, and the disclosure ledger are designed, not built.

09 · Architectural properties

What an architecture review will ask, answered.

Audit

Tamper-evident by construction

Every ruling and memory entry is an immutable, content-addressed version in one hash-chained ledger. Making tampering impossible, not only evident, is a deployment choice: retention-locked object storage, customer-managed keys, anchored ledger heads.

Agent boundary

Agents read; people decide

The MCP server lets an agent search memory, list candidates and run approved investigations. Its tool contract requires that a decision come from the human the client acts for. Enforcing that with the SSO identity is designed, not built.

Integration

Additive to the stack you have

Today it reads SQL query history and exports an Apache Ossie model. Reading the drafts your warehouse generates and publishing rulings back to semantic layers and BI are designed, not built. No warehouse, BI or model replacement.

Ownership

Lent to models, never trained in

A model reads the relevant rulings for one question and keeps nothing. Swap models and the memory stays. Redact a ruling and no future answer can use it.

Disclosure

You choose the exposure, and it is recorded

Works with a hosted API, a private endpoint in your cloud account, or a model you run yourself. A disclosure ledger (designed) records which entries went to which client, by hash, never by content.

Conformance

Checks agents you did not build

Every query in the warehouse log, human or agent, can be compared to the ruling in force when it ran. No model call, nothing leaves. Designed; it needs execution-backed comparison before it ships.

Records management

Deletion is a version

Redaction scrubs content and keeps actor and timestamp. Writes carry a content-hash precondition, so two reviewers cannot silently overwrite each other.

Hosting

Self-hosted, Apache-2.0

Runs inside your boundary. The test suite runs offline with no API key; live model calls go through a spend ledger with an enforced ceiling.

10 · Evidence to date

Tested to fail, and reported when it does.

All evidence is offline and deterministic, on TPC-H, TPC-DS and a synthetic order-to-cash world. Reproducible from a clean clone at the tag paper-v0.1.

Finding

Frequency wins silently when the review step only sees the merged view.

While building the test, the induction stage merged rival definitions by intersecting their filters. The disputed filter was dropped, so only the majority's definition reached memory and there was nothing to rule on. Accept-or-reject review has the same weakness by design: it cannot recover a minority definition it was never shown.

11 · The opportunity

A pilot sized to one domain and a handful of owners.

The measurement still missing is the one a buyer cares about: whether governed memory improves answers on real questions. It needs real query history and people with the authority to rule. Proving grounds are domains where small definitional errors cost visible money: fulfillment, revenue operations, supply chain.

Input

One domain's query history

Plus the drafts your warehouse already generates. Read-only access, inside your boundary.

People

A few named owners, about 20 rulings

Owners rule only where a fork appears, as part of asking ordinary questions. Nobody pre-authors definitions.

Measure

Time to a trusted definition

Before and after, plus whether reviewers accept the system's reasoning when they inspect it.

Your institutional memory stays yours.

Models borrow it one question at a time, and nothing is trained in. It is built so that every ruling can be revoked, every loan is recorded, and every agent's output is checked against your rules inside your own boundary, by code that sends nothing out.
